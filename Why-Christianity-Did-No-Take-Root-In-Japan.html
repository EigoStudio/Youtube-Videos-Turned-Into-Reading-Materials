Part 1: Foreign Trade as a Gateway and the Strategic Conversion of Daimyo

In 1549, Francis Xavier landed in Kagoshima, marking the formal introduction of Roman Catholicism to Japan. This arrival coincided with the chaotic Sengoku period, an era dominated by relentless warfare among regional warlords (daimyo). Xavier’s mission was part of a larger global counter-offensive orchestrated by the Catholic Church. Following the Protestant Reformation triggered by Martin Luther in Europe, the Papacy sought to recover its diminished prestige by expanding its congregation into Asia and the Americas. Under papal treaties, Catholic powers like Portugal and Spain embarked on maritime exploration, where religious proselytization was inextricably bound up with trade and imperial conquest.

Initially, foreign missionaries aimed their conversion efforts at commoners, but they quickly discerned that winning over ruling lords yielded exponentially greater results. This pragmatic approach accelerated the rise of Christian daimyo (Kirishitan daimyo), such as Omura Sumitada. The primary impetus for these warlords to convert was rarely pure spiritual conviction; instead, it was overwhelmingly commercial. By receiving baptism, daimyo secured lucrative trade privileges with Portuguese merchants, gaining access to advanced Western technology, clocks, firearms, and critically, saltpeter—an indispensable ingredient for gunpowder during wartime.

However, this top-down conversion strategy unleashed severe social unrest. In Omura domain, Sumitada coerced his subjects into adopting Christianity and commanded the demolition of traditional Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines. Sumitada went so far as to donate the vital port city of Nagasaki directly to the Society of Jesus. Meanwhile, powerful warlords like Oda Nobunaga observed the missionaries with calculated tolerance. Nobunaga patronized Jesuits like Luis Frois, not because he intended to embrace Catholicism, but because he sought to utilize Christianity as a strategic counterweight against militant Buddhist sects that posed a direct challenge to his political hegemony.

【Part 2 学習画面】

Part 2: Human Trafficking and Hideyoshi’s Decisive Action

Following Oda Nobunaga’s assassination in 1582, Toyotomi Hideyoshi successfully unified Japan. At first, Hideyoshi maintained a tolerant attitude toward foreign trade, fully acknowledging its economic and military utility. However, his perception shifted radically during his military expedition to Kyushu in 1587. Hideyoshi was horrified to discover that thousands of Japanese citizens were being purchased by European traders and trafficked overseas as slaves. Furthermore, he learned that the strategic port of Nagasaki had been ceded to the Jesuits and heavily fortified with artillery.

Tensions flared when the Jesuit Vice-Provincial, Gaspar Coelho, displayed a formidable armed warship to Hideyoshi at Hakata. Coelho had previously dispatched letters proposing that converted Japanese soldiers be utilized as mercenaries to assist Spain in conquering Ming Dynasty China. Recognizing these actions as unambiguous signs of European colonial expansion, Hideyoshi confronted Coelho with four direct demands, insisting on the immediate prohibition of human trafficking, the cessation of forced conversions, and the protection of native religious shrines.

Dissatisfied with the missionaries' evasive justifications, Hideyoshi promulgated the Edict of Expulsion of Missionaries (Bateren Tsuihou-rei) in 1587. Although he permitted commercial trade to continue and refrained from persecuting low-ranking Japanese believers, foreign priests were ordered to vacate the country. Hostilities peaked in 1596 during the San Felipe Incident, when a shipwrecked Spanish captain allegedly declared that Spain deployed missionaries to infiltrate nations prior to military invasion. Enraged, Hideyoshi executed 26 foreign priests and Japanese Christians in Nagasaki in 1597, demonstrating that Japan would aggressively defend its territorial sovereignty.

Part 3: Tokugawa Hegemony and the Road to National Isolation

Following Hideyoshi’s death, Tokugawa Ieyasu established the Tokugawa Shogunate in 1603. Ieyasu initially harbored ambitious plans to expand international commerce, courting trade not only with Spain and Portugal, but also with Protestant powers like England and the Netherlands. The Dutch and English assured Ieyasu that they sought strictly commercial relations without any intention of spreading religion. This distinction was critical for the Shogunate, as it allowed Japan to maintain profitable foreign trade while eliminating the ideological threat associated with Catholic proselytization.

However, internal security concerns quickly overshadowed Ieyasu’s commercial ambitions. The Shogunate grew deeply alarmed by the absolute devotion that Japanese Christians pledged to God and the Pope, which directly subverted the feudal loyalty owed to the Shogun. Fearing foreign-backed subversion, the Shogunate issued nationwide edicts banning Christianity in 1612. Churches were systematically demolished, foreign missionaries were expelled or executed, and Japanese converts were subjected to brutal persecution unless they formally renounced their faith.

The ultimate climax occurred in 1637 with the Shimabara Rebellion, a massive armed revolt led by Christian peasants protesting severe religious oppression and exorbitant taxation. Though the rebellion was suppressed after heavy casualties, it convinced the Shogunate that Christianity was a dangerous catalyst for domestic rebellion. In response, the Shogunate finalized the National Isolation policy (Sakoku). By 1639, Portuguese ships were permanently banned, leaving only the Dutch restricted to the artificial island of Dejima in Nagasaki under intense state surveillance for the next 250 years.

【Part 4 学習画面】

Part 4: Conclusion: Why Japan Successfully Safeguarded Its Independence

The failure of Christianity to take root in Japan was not a mere historical coincidence; rather, it was the direct outcome of a sophisticated defensive strategy implemented by Japan’s rulers. During the 16th and 17th centuries, numerous nations throughout Asia, Africa, and the Americas fell victim to European colonization. Typically, Western powers used missionary work to penetrate societies, convert the populace, and subsequently deploy military forces to overthrow local governments. Japan successfully resisted this fate through formidable military strength, accurate intelligence gathering, and resolute decision-making.

During the Sengoku era, Japan possessed an extraordinary military capacity. European observers, including Jesuit Inspector Alessandro Valignano, recorded in reports that Japanese samurai were remarkably courageous and highly trained in warfare. Furthermore, Japan maintained one of the largest concentrations of firearms in the world. Recognizing that a direct military invasion of Japan was practically impossible, European colonial powers relied on religious conversion as an indirect tool for conquest. Japanese leaders clearly perceived this strategy and neutralized the threat before foreign powers could establish a foothold.

Ultimately, Japanese rulers demonstrated extraordinary strategic flexibility. They actively engaged in foreign trade when it served the national interest, but fearlessly executed national isolation when sovereignty was endangered. By enforcing Sakoku, Japan preserved its political autonomy, preventing Western colonization and allowing a unique, peaceful culture to flourish throughout the Edo period. This historical episode highlights the vital importance of military readiness, pragmatic leadership, and geopolitical awareness in preserving national independence.
